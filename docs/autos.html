Auto loans: same score, different lender

Data through July 2026. Public ABS-EE loan-level filings on EDGAR: 759,808 loans in 10 deals from 7 lenders.

These are loans inside public auto ABS trusts, not the lenders' whole books. A loan enters when the trust is formed or replenished and leaves when it pays off, is repurchased, or charges off; some sponsors drop paid-off loans from the file the month after payoff and others keep them, which the exit rule handles. Age is months since origination, and a loan counts only from its fourth month in the trust: it had to be current to be sold in, so its first months there can hardly end in a charge-off. Scores are the score type the sponsor reports (FICO at most captives, a bureau score at Santander, VantageScore at Exeter for 98.9% of its pool) and are never refreshed after origination, so a 700 at Exeter and a 700 at Honda are close but not the same number.

What the data says

Deep in subprime, scores 540 to 599. Charge-off by 24 months, averaged over the buckets every subprime lender has there: exeter 21.8%, santander 15.1%, americredit 12.9%. Highest against lowest: 1.7 times (95% interval 1.5 to 1.8). Cells need at least 1,500 loans and 250 still under observation at 24 months.

Who lends matters about as much as what the score says. Comparing lenders inside one 20-point bucket at a time, across the 5 buckets from 660 to 759 where at least three lenders overlap and a second lender has 10 or more charge-offs by twelve months, the highest one-year charge-off rate is a median 16 times the lowest (95% interval 10 to 19); the bucket ratios run from 9 to 17. In the 660 to 679 bucket that is exeter at 5.64% against carmax at 0.63%. In some of these buckets capone, honda and toyota sit lower still, on fewer than 10 charge-offs each by twelve months, which is too few to divide by. The 760 and 780 buckets have three lenders but no second one with 10 charge-offs, so no ratio. Inside a single lender, moving a borrower from scores 500-599 to 760-839 moves the monthly odds by about 9 times (section 3, score specification). Counting each loan from its first month in the trust instead, as this page did until November 2026, the median under the same rule is 13 times, and from its seventh month 11. The page then printed 50 times, because every one of its ratios divided by a cell with one to three charge-offs. Checks: without Exeter, whose score is VantageScore rather than FICO, the median is 9 times (95% interval 8 to 15) across 5 buckets, outside the interval above: Exeter is the highest lender in the 660, 680 and 700 buckets, so part of the gap is a comparison across score scales; keeping only the 5 lenders whose scores run past 850, the 250-900 auto-industry scale (capone, carmax, honda, santander, toyota), the median is 9 times (95% interval 8 to 15); americredit and exeter top out at 850 or just above, and the result is the same as without Exeter alone, because the other lender off that scale is never the highest or the lowest lender with enough charge-offs; comparing the same two lenders in the same bucket inside a single origination year, the ratio is 0.93 of the pooled one (95% interval 0.90 to 1.02), median over 29 pairs (section 2). The intervals resample loans within each lender, so they carry no deal-to-deal luck, which section 3 measures separately. The lender a borrower ends up at is not recorded in any credit file. This is a description of who defaults where, not an effect of the lender.

Holding the bureau score and the loan structure fixed, lenders still differ by several times. Against the baseline lender, the monthly charge-off odds run from 0.15 at honda to 1.10 at exeter. Some of the raw gap is composition: at honda the odds ratio moves from 0.04 with age alone to 0.13 once score is held fixed. A large gap survives both. In the terms column, against santander: honda 0.15, toyota 0.19, carmax 0.23, capone 0.36, americredit 0.75, exeter 1.10. It is not a causal effect of the lender: it carries what each lender knows beyond the score, who it attracts, how it services, and which loans it puts in a public deal. Each lender's coefficient also carries the luck of its own deals, and 4 of these lenders own a single deal, so their clustered intervals measure only noise inside that deal. Measured where it can be, between the two deals of each lender that has two, the deal-to-deal spread is about 0.33 in log odds (3 degrees of freedom). With that spread added to each interval, honda 0.15 [0.04, 0.61], toyota 0.19 [0.05, 0.77], carmax 0.23 [0.06, 0.89] remain distinguishable from santander, and capone 0.36 [0.09, 1.46], americredit 0.75 [0.18, 3.07], exeter 1.10 [0.31, 3.85] are not.

The lender's own price carries part of what the score carries. Scores 500 to 599 have 9.1 times the monthly charge-off odds of scores 760 to 839 with age and lender held fixed, 6.8 times once loan structure is held fixed too, and 4.0 times once the lender's APR quintile is added. So price removes about 27% of the score gradient that is left after loan structure, in log odds. Price is set by the lender from its own read of the borrower, so that column answers a narrower question and is not the preferred specification.

Top loan amount quintile against the bottom, at the same score, lender and origination quarter: odds ratio 0.72 [0.64, 0.81], interval excludes 1.

Top payment-to-income quintile against the bottom, at the same score, lender and origination quarter: odds ratio 2.47 [2.03, 3.01], interval excludes 1.

Top loan-to-value quintile against the bottom, at the same score, lender and origination quarter: odds ratio 4.24 [3.35, 5.38], interval excludes 1.

Pricing cutoffs mostly are not clean experiments. Of 16 candidate score cutoffs examined, 8 fail the density test, meaning the pool itself changes at the line and the cutoff is an approval or securitization threshold rather than a pricing step. 7 have both a smooth density and a real jump in APR or loan amount. 1 of them clears two conventional standard errors at 24 months. With 32 tests run across cutoffs and horizons, about 2 would clear that bar by chance alone, so it is not treated as a finding; the table in section 4 gives every estimate so the reader can judge. With the bias-corrected robust intervals rdrobust reports, 0 of 14 cutoff-and-horizon estimates clear zero at the optimal bandwidth. Across the 56 refits at 0.5, 0.75, 1.5 and 2 times that bandwidth, 4 clear zero (honda 700 at 24 months, 2 times; honda 605 at 12 months, 2 times; honda 605 at 24 months, 2 times; santander 725 at 12 months, 0.5 times), about the 3 that chance alone would give at the 5% level, and 0 of 14 stay clear at every bandwidth. This is the only causal design on the page, and its answer is a null.

What kind of claim each section makes. Sections 2, 3 and 5 are descriptive: who defaults, at which lender, holding what fixed. Controls narrow a comparison, they do not make it causal, because a lender's borrowers differ in ways no field in the filing records. Section 4 is the only design here that could carry a causal sentence, and only at a cutoff that passes its screens.

2. Cumulative charge-off by score bucket, by lender

Age is months since origination, with delayed entry: a loan counts in the risk set only at ages it was actually observed in the trust. Aalen-Johansen cumulative incidence treats prepayment and repurchase as competing exits; the Kaplan-Meier version treats them as censoring and is always higher. Buckets are 20 points wide, labelled by the lower edge; cells with fewer than 500 loans are not drawn. Bands are 95 percent percentile bootstrap over loans.

The pool window. A loan has to be current to be sold into a trust, so in its first months there it can hardly charge off: in the hazard model of section 3 the charge-off odds in a loan's first three months in the pool are 0.12 times a seasoned loan's, and in months 3 to 5 still 0.66 times. A lender whose loans enter old spends much of its first year in the data inside that window. Median age at entry, in months, one figure per deal: exeter 3, santander 3, carmax 3 and 5, americredit 9, honda 10, capone 13 and toyota 13. So every curve and table in this section, and every lender ratio in the summary above, counts a loan only from its fourth month in the trust; a loan that leaves before then is not counted. That removes most of the window; months 3 to 5 remain, so lenders whose loans enter old still look somewhat safer here than they would if every loan were followed from origination. Until November 2026 the page counted loans from their first month in the trust.

Inside one origination year at a time

A lender's public pools hold one or two vintages, and vintages differ across the industry, so a pooled lender gap could be a vintage gap. Here each comparison is inside one origination year and one 20-point bucket. Same cell rule as above: at least 1,500 loans and 250 still observed at the horizon.

The test: take two lenders that both have a cell in the same bucket inside one origination year, and compare their charge-off ratio there with their ratio when every vintage is pooled. If the pooled gap were a vintage gap, the ratio inside the year would sit nearer one. Over 29 such pairs the within-year ratio is a median 0.93 of the pooled ratio. 3. Monthly charge-off hazard: lender at fixed score, size at fixed score and lender

Discrete-time competing-risks hazard on the loan-month expansion, fitted as binomial logits on aggregated cells with standard errors clustered by deal and t critical values on nine degrees of freedom (ten deals); lender intervals also carry the deal-to-deal spread (headline above). Odds ratios are relative to a stated baseline level. Four specifications, each adding one layer: raw is loan age only; score adds the bureau score bucket; terms adds origination quarter, loan amount, payment-to-income and loan-to-value; price adds the lender's own APR quintile. Price is a mediator, not a confounder. The lender sets it from its private read of the borrower, so conditioning on it answers the narrower question of whether two lenders who priced a borrower the same then see the same losses. Read terms as the main result and price as a decomposition.

5. Selection or protection: where in the process the advantage sits

A lender that loses less at the same score is either picking borrowers who do not get into trouble, or holding an account that survives trouble better. Those are different claims and the level of charge-off cannot separate them, so the gap is split into two stages: entry, the chance of reaching 30 days past due within 24 months of origination, and roll, the chance of charging off within 12 months of first reaching 30 days. Their product approximates the charge-off rate, and the log gap against the baseline lender splits between them. It leaves out charge-offs never recorded 30 days late, bankruptcy-type events coded straight to charge-off: 75% of capone's, so the split says little about capone, and at most 6.5% at every other lender.

The roll comparison is the more trustworthy of the two. It conditions on an observed behaviour, being 30 days late, rather than on a score, so it is not disturbed by the fact that these lenders report different score types on different scales. The entry comparison is partly contaminated by that scale difference; the roll comparison is not.

For carmax, honda, toyota the advantage is selection. Median across score buckets: carmax (entry 103% of the gap, roll ratio 1.07); honda (entry 84% of the gap, roll ratio 0.75); toyota (entry 86% of the gap, roll ratio 0.76). Their borrowers are far less likely to fall 30 days behind at the same reported score, and once behind they charge off at close to the baseline lender's rate. There is no sign here of an account that borrowers protect: the protection would have to show up in the roll, and it does not. For exeter the difference sits in the roll instead. Median: exeter (roll ratio 1.41, entry ratio 1.00). Borrowers reach 30 days late at close to the baseline rate but the outcome after that differs, which points at workout practice, repossession policy, or the value of the collateral to the borrower rather than at who was lent to. Neither pattern is a within-person comparison, so none of it measures how a borrower ranks two debts against each other. What it does rule out is the simplest version of the protection story for the lenders whose entire gap is entry: their accounts do not survive trouble better, they just meet trouble less often.

6. What this does and does not say about cards

Nothing here observes a person's card and auto loan together, so it says nothing about which one they pay first. It says how default varies with lender and loan size at a fixed score in one product where the loan-level data is public. The score-shape of default from section 2 is one of the three candidate shapes used on the cards page.
