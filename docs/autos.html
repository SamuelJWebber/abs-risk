Auto loans: same score, different lender

Built 2026-09-08 from public ABS-EE loan-level filings on EDGAR. 759,808 loans in 10 deals from 7 lenders.

These are loans inside public auto ABS trusts, not the lenders' whole books. A loan enters when the trust is formed or replenished and leaves when it pays off, is repurchased, or charges off; some sponsors drop paid-off loans from the file the month after payoff and others keep them, which the exit rule handles. Months are counted from the first month the loan is seen in the trust. Scores are the score type the sponsor reports (FICO at most captives, a bureau score at Santander, VantageScore at Exeter) and are never refreshed after origination.

What the data says

Deep in subprime, scores 480 to 639. Charge-off by 24 months, averaged over the buckets each lender has: exeter 20.80%, santander 13.77%, americredit 10.10%. Highest against lowest, at the same bureau score: 2 times. Cells need at least 1,500 loans and 250 still under observation at 24 months.

Where subprime and prime lenders overlap, scores 700 to 779. Charge-off by 12 months, averaged over the buckets each lender has: exeter 5.80%, santander 2.85%, carmax 0.23%, toyota 0.13%, capone 0.09%, honda 0.09%. Highest against lowest, at the same bureau score: 61 times. Cells need at least 1,500 loans and 250 still under observation at 12 months.

Who lends matters more than what the score says. Comparing lenders inside one 20-point bucket at a time, across the 7 buckets from 660 to 799 where at least three lenders overlap, the highest one-year charge-off rate is 50 times the lowest (range 4 to 73). In the 660 to 679 bucket that is exeter at 5.06% against honda at 0.10%. Inside a single lender, moving a borrower from the 500s to the 800s moves the monthly odds by about nine times. The lender a borrower ends up at is not a small effect beside that, and no credit file records it.

Holding the bureau score and the loan structure fixed, lenders still differ by several times. Against the baseline lender, the monthly charge-off odds run from 0.16 at honda to 1.13 at exeter. Some of the raw gap is composition: at honda the odds ratio moves from 0.04 with age alone to 0.11 once score is held fixed. A large gap survives both. It is not a causal effect of the lender: it carries what each lender knows beyond the score, who it attracts, how it services, and which loans it puts in a public deal.

The lender's own price carries much of what the score carries. Scores 500 to 599 have 9 times the monthly charge-off odds of scores 760 to 839 with age and lender held fixed, 7 times once loan structure is held fixed too, and 4.1 times once the lender's APR quintile is added. Adding price removes about 37% of the remaining score gradient in log odds. Price is set by the lender from its own read of the borrower, so that column answers a narrower question and is not the preferred specification.

Top loan amount quintile against the bottom, at the same score, lender and origination quarter: odds ratio 0.72 [0.65, 0.80], interval excludes 1.

Top payment-to-income quintile against the bottom, at the same score, lender and origination quarter: odds ratio 2.47 [2.08, 2.94], interval excludes 1.

Top loan-to-value quintile against the bottom, at the same score, lender and origination quarter: odds ratio 4.33 [3.48, 5.38], interval excludes 1.

Pricing cutoffs mostly are not clean experiments. Of 16 candidate score cutoffs examined, 8 fail the density test, meaning the pool itself changes at the line and the cutoff is an approval or securitization threshold rather than a pricing step. 7 have both a smooth density and a real jump in APR. 1 of them clears two standard errors at 24 months. With 32 tests run across cutoffs and horizons, about 2 would clear that bar by chance alone, so it is not treated as a finding; the table in section 4 gives every estimate so the reader can judge.

3. Monthly charge-off hazard: lender at fixed score, size at fixed score and lender

Discrete-time competing-risks hazard on the loan-month expansion, fitted as binomial logits on aggregated cells with standard errors clustered by deal. Odds ratios are relative to a stated baseline level. Four specifications, each adding one layer: raw is loan age only; score adds the bureau score bucket; terms adds origination quarter, loan amount, payment-to-income and loan-to-value; price adds the lender's own APR quintile. Price is a mediator, not a confounder. The lender sets it from its private read of the borrower, so conditioning on it answers the narrower question of whether two lenders who priced a borrower the same then see the same losses. Read terms as the main result and price as a decomposition.

4. Regression discontinuity at lender pricing cutoffs

Where a lender's APR or loan amount jumps at a score, loans just above and below the line are alike except for the terms they got. A cutoff counts only if the jump exists (first stage), the score density does not pile up on one side (McCrary test), and it is not a known securitization floor. Charge-off effects are local-linear with an MSE-optimal bandwidth and bias-corrected robust confidence intervals.

5. Selection or protection: where in the process the advantage sits

A lender that loses less at the same score is either picking borrowers who do not get into trouble, or holding an account that survives trouble better. Those are different claims and the level of charge-off cannot separate them, so the gap is split into two stages: entry, the chance of reaching 30 days past due within 24 months of origination, and roll, the chance of charging off within 12 months of first reaching 30 days. Their product is the charge-off rate, so the log gap against the baseline lender splits cleanly between them.

The roll comparison is the more trustworthy of the two. It conditions on an observed behaviour, being 30 days late, rather than on a score, so it is not disturbed by the fact that these lenders report different score types on different scales. The entry comparison is partly contaminated by that scale difference; the roll comparison is not.

For carmax, honda, toyota the advantage is selection. Median across score buckets: carmax (entry 103% of the gap, roll ratio 1.07); honda (entry 84% of the gap, roll ratio 0.75); toyota (entry 86% of the gap, roll ratio 0.76). Their borrowers are far less likely to fall 30 days behind at the same reported score, and once behind they charge off at close to the baseline lender's rate. There is no sign here of an account that borrowers protect: the protection would have to show up in the roll, and it does not. For exeter the difference sits in the roll instead. Median: exeter (roll ratio 1.41, entry ratio 1.00). Borrowers reach 30 days late at close to the baseline rate but the outcome after that differs, which points at workout practice, repossession policy, or the value of the collateral to the borrower rather than at who was lent to. Neither pattern is a within-person comparison, so none of it measures how a borrower ranks two debts against each other. What it does rule out is the simplest version of the protection story for the lenders whose entire gap is entry: their accounts do not survive trouble better, they just meet trouble less often.

6. What this does and does not say about cards

Nothing here observes a person's card and auto loan together, so it says nothing about which one they pay first. It says how default varies with lender and loan size at a fixed score in one product where the loan-level data is public. The score-shape of default from section 2 is one of the three candidate shapes used on the cards page.
