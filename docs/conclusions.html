Does anyone pay one card first?

Card data through September 2026; auto data through July 2026. 561 monthly trust reports and 759,808 auto loans across 10 deals and 7 lenders, all from public SEC filings.

The question

American Express loses less on its card portfolio than Synchrony does. Toyota loses less on its auto loans than Santander does. Both facts survive holding the borrower's credit score fixed. There are two very different reasons that could be true, and they have opposite implications for anyone pricing credit:

Selection. The issuer picked people who do not miss payments, seeing something the bureau score does not. Priority. The same people miss payments as often, but pay that account first when money is short.

The loss rate cannot tell them apart, because both predict the same lower number. Almost every public comparison of issuers stops there and calls it underwriting quality.

The test

A card trust's monthly charge-off rate factors exactly into three shares the trust reports itself, and the two stories load onto different ones:

monthly charge-off  =  entry, the share of balances 30 or more days late  x  progression, the share of those that are 90 or more days late  x  conversion, the month's charge-off as a share of balances 90 or more days late

Selection moves entry and leaves conversion alone: the borrower rarely gets into trouble, and once in trouble resolves like anyone else. Priority moves conversion: the borrower gets into trouble at the normal rate and rescues that account. Priority could lower entry too, since a borrower who pays a card first falls behind on it less often, so entry alone does not separate the stories; conversion is the measure that can, and it is also the more trustworthy one, because it conditions on an observed behaviour, already being 90 days late, rather than on a credit score whose scale differs between lenders. Progression is close to the same at every trust.

The answer: selection, on every level comparison

Cards. amex loses 0.37 times what synchrony loses each month. Its share of balances 30 days late is 0.29 times synchrony's. But once an account is 90 days late, it converts to a write-off at 1.39 times synchrony's rate. Entry accounts for 125% of the gap, progression for +8% and conversion for -33%. Priority predicts the opposite sign on conversion: a protected card would rescue its late accounts, which is a low conversion rate, not a high one.

This decomposition is arithmetic, not a model. Each column is a geometric mean over the trust's months, so for every row entry x progression x conversion is co_monthly, and the three shares of the gap against synchrony sum to one.

Autos, where the data is loan by loan. Comparing lenders inside the same 20-point score bucket, carmax, honda, toyota owe 84% to 103% of their advantage to entry. Their borrowers are several times less likely to reach 30 days past due; once past due they charge off at close to the baseline lender's rate (roll ratios carmax 1.07, honda 0.75, toyota 0.76).

One instructive exception. exeter: entry ratio 1.00, roll ratio 1.41. Its borrowers fall behind at the same rate as the baseline lender's but lose far more often afterwards. That is a difference in what happens after trouble starts, which is where priority would live, but for a secured product the likelier readings are repossession policy and workout practice rather than the borrower's ranking of debts.

A second test, on direction rather than level. If people rank their debts, the ranking should bite hardest when money is short, so a protected card's conversion ratio should fall when delinquency is high. Split the 94 months by the trusts' own delinquency. In the 47 months when it ran above its median (1.16 times each trust's usual 30-plus share, against 0.84 in the other 47), the conversion ratio against synchrony fell for amex (-0.10), rose for citi (+0.09), and moved by less than 0.05 for bofa, chase and comet. For amex, the lowest-loss trust, it went from 1.44 in loose months to 1.34 in tight ones: the direction prioritisation predicts, while staying above synchrony's 1.00. Of the named periods, the step from normalising 2022-23H1 to squeeze 2023H2 on raised every trust's 30-plus share (by 16% to 56%). Across it the conversion ratio fell for bofa (-0.12) and comet (-0.06), rose for none, and was flat for amex, chase and citi. The first and last named periods are not a tightening: every trust's 30-plus share is lower in squeeze 2023H2 on than in pre-covid 2019 (0.74 to 0.98 times). Until November 2026 this page compared exactly those two and read the result as a test of prioritisation; it was not one. So the direction test is mixed. The moves either way are small beside the level gap above, and conversion divides a flow by a stock that builds ahead of the charge-offs, which lowers it whenever delinquency is rising, whoever is paid first. The level comparison carries the argument; this test neither confirms nor overturns it.

What else the data settled

The size of a loan is not the risk in it; the payment is. At the same score, lender and origination quarter, comparing the top quintile against the bottom:

A bigger loan at the same burden defaults less (0.72), because at a fixed payment and a fixed loan-to-value a larger balance means a larger income behind it. What raises default is the monthly burden (2.47) and lending past the value of the collateral (4.24). Anyone reasoning about a bigger line as risk in itself has the wrong variable.

How much of an issuer's loss rate is its customer mix is genuinely unknown. Predicting each trust's losses from its own score mix needs a loss curve by score, and no public source publishes one for cards. The four published curves that exist disagree by about five times. Under all four, the ends of the ranking hold (amex, citi, synchrony keep their place), but 5 of 6 trusts change the sign of their gap depending on which curve is assumed. The ordering of the extremes is a finding. The size of anyone's gap is not.

What this cannot say

The conversion comparison rules out the simple priority story, because priority would show up as accounts rescued from deep delinquency and the opposite is observed. It does not separate the borrower's behaviour from the issuer's: an issuer that charges off faster, or re-ages less, produces the same high conversion rate. Separating those needs each issuer's charge-off policy, which the filings do not state.

Nothing here observes one person holding two accounts. That is the measurement payment hierarchy actually requires, and it does not exist in public data: Regulation AB II exempted credit card securitisations from loan-level disclosure, and the credit panels that could answer it are confidential. Anyone claiming to have measured hierarchy from public filings has measured something else.

Three further limits, stated rather than buried. Trust pools are the securitised, seasoned end of an issuer's book and not the book. Lenders report different score models on different scales, which is why the argument leans on conversion rather than on entry. And the regression discontinuity designs at lenders' own pricing cutoffs returned mostly nulls: half the candidate cutoffs failed a density test, meaning the applicant pool changes at the line, and the survivors were underpowered.

The short version

Issuers with low losses at the same credit score got there by lending to people who do not fall behind, not by holding an account their customers protect: their late accounts are not rescued more often than anyone else's. The advantage is bought at origination. The timing of losses across the cycle neither confirms nor overturns that. For a lender, that means the returns to better selection dwarf the returns to being someone's favourite card. For a borrower, it means the card that looks safest is safest because of who else holds it.
