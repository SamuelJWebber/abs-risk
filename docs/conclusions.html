Does anyone pay one card first?

Card data through October 2026; auto data through July 2026. 567 monthly trust reports and 759,808 auto loans across 10 deals and 7 lenders, all from public SEC filings.

The question

The American Express card trust loses less than Synchrony's. Toyota's public auto loan pool loses less than Santander's, and that one holds with the borrower's credit score fixed; for the card trusts, whether it does depends on which loss curve is assumed. These are one securitised trust per card issuer and one or two public deals per auto lender, not the lenders' whole books. There are two very different reasons a lender's losses could be lower, and they have opposite implications for anyone pricing credit:

Selection. The issuer picked people who do not miss payments, seeing something the bureau score does not. Priority. The same people miss payments as often, but pay that account first when money is short.

The loss rate cannot tell them apart, because both predict the same lower number. Almost every public comparison of issuers stops there and calls it underwriting quality.

What can be measured

A card trust's monthly charge-off rate factors exactly into three shares the trust reports itself:

monthly charge-off  =  entry, the share of balances 30 or more days late  x  progression, the share of those that are 90 or more days late  x  conversion, the month's charge-off as a share of balances 90 or more days late

Entry is where both stories speak, and they say the same thing: an issuer that selected well has few accounts falling behind, and so does an issuer whose card is paid first. The stories part only on what happens to an account once it is late. Conversion looks like that measure and is not one: its numerator is every charge-off in the month, including accounts written off on bankruptcy or death that were never 90 days late. Progression is close to the same at every trust.

What the filings show: far fewer late balances, and nothing on rescue

Cards. amex loses 0.29 times what synchrony loses each month, after recoveries. Its share of balances 30 days late is 0.29 times synchrony's. Entry accounts for 101% of the gap, progression for +6% and conversion for -7%. So the gap in losses is a gap in how many balances are late at all. Selection predicts that, and so does a card that is paid first.

This decomposition is arithmetic, not a model. Each column is a geometric mean over the trust's months, on the net charge-off rate every trust prints, so for every row entry x progression x conversion is co_monthly, and the three shares of the gap against synchrony sum to one.

Why conversion says nothing about rescue. An account reaches the 180-day write-off only from the last delinquency bucket of the month before, so if every charge-off had aged through delinquency a month's charge-offs could not exceed what that bucket held. They do: chase wrote off 1.21 times the dollars in that bucket, so at least 17% of its charge-offs never aged through late delinquency; amex, whose last bucket holds 2 monthly cohorts, about 1.37 times one of them (27%). The prospectuses say why: accounts are written off at about 180 days late, or sooner when the cardholder is bankrupt or has died. A conversion ratio therefore mixes aged and unaged losses in a proportion no monthly report gives. A ratio above one is not evidence that an issuer's late accounts fare worse, and a ratio below one is not evidence that they are rescued. Until November 2026 this page read American Express's ratio as the opposite of protection and concluded that the answer was selection. That did not follow, and for cards it is withdrawn. The check for every trust is on the cards page, section 5.

Autos, where the data is loan by loan. Comparing lenders inside the same 20-point score bucket, carmax, honda, toyota owe 81% to 98% of their advantage to entry: their borrowers are several times less likely to reach 30 days past due. Once past due the difference is smaller and uneven: median roll ratios against the baseline lender are carmax 0.96, honda 0.61, toyota 0.70, and the ratio is below one in these score buckets: carmax in 6 of 7, honda in 9 of 9, toyota in 6 of 7. So most of the advantage is in not falling behind. For some lenders a smaller part is in what happens afterwards, which for a secured loan is as likely to be repossession and workout practice as the borrower's ranking of debts.

One instructive exception. exeter: entry ratio 1.00, roll ratio 1.24. Its borrowers fall behind at about the same rate as the baseline lender's but default more often afterwards. That is a difference in what happens after trouble starts, and the likelier readings are again repossession policy and workout practice.

A test on direction rather than level. If people rank their debts, the ranking should bite hardest when money is short, so a protected card's conversion ratio should fall when delinquency is high. Split the 95 months by the trusts' own delinquency. In the 47 months when it ran above its median (1.16 times each trust's usual 30-plus share, against 0.85 in the other 48), the conversion ratio against synchrony fell for no trust, rose for amex (+0.08) and comet (+0.09), and moved by less than 0.05 for bofa, chase and citi. For amex, the lowest-loss trust, it went from 1.05 in loose months to 1.13 in tight ones: the wrong way for prioritisation, while staying above synchrony's 1.00. Of the named periods, the step from normalising 2022-23H1 to squeeze 2023H2 on raised every trust's 30-plus share (by 16% to 56%). Across it the conversion ratio fell for citi (-0.06), rose for amex (+0.08) and comet (+0.09), and was flat for bofa and chase. The first and last named periods are not a tightening: every trust's 30-plus share is lower in squeeze 2023H2 on than in pre-covid 2019 (0.74 to 0.98 times). Until November 2026 this page compared exactly those two and read the result as a test of prioritisation; it was not one. No trust's conversion ratio fell in the tight months, which is not the sign prioritisation predicts. Conversion divides a flow by a stock that builds ahead of the charge-offs, which lowers it whenever delinquency is rising, whoever is paid first, and it moves with the share of charge-offs that never aged through late delinquency. So this test is weak evidence in either direction and settles nothing on its own.

What else the data shows

In these auto pools the payment carries the risk, not the size of the loan. At the same score, lender and origination quarter, comparing the top quintile against the bottom:

A bigger loan at the same burden defaults less (0.72), because at a fixed payment and a fixed loan-to-value a larger balance means a larger income behind it. What goes with more default is the monthly burden (2.51) and lending past the value of the collateral (4.25). These compare loans that differ in one recorded field. They are not the effect of lending the same borrower more, and they say nothing about card limits, which no data here observes.

How much of an issuer's loss rate is its customer mix is unknown. Predicting each trust's losses from its own score mix needs a loss curve by score, and no public source publishes one for cards. The four published curves that exist disagree by about five times. Across the 12 combinations of loss curve and straddle rule, synchrony has the largest gap in 10 and amex the smallest in 10, but 5 of 6 trusts change the sign of their gap depending on which curve is assumed. The size of anyone's gap is not a finding.

What this cannot say

It cannot say whether a low-loss issuer's late accounts are rescued more or less often than anyone else's. That is the one place the two stories differ, and the monthly reports do not show it. The prospectuses do state each issuer's charge-off policy, about 180 days late or sooner on bankruptcy or death, but no report splits the charge-offs that aged through delinquency from the ones written off early, and an issuer that charges off faster or re-ages less would move the same numbers.

Nothing here observes one person holding two accounts. That is the measurement payment hierarchy actually requires, and it does not exist in public data: Regulation AB II exempted credit card securitisations from loan-level disclosure, and the credit panels that could answer it are confidential. Anyone claiming to have measured hierarchy from public filings has measured something else.

Three further limits, stated rather than buried. Trust pools are the securitised, seasoned end of an issuer's book and not the book. Lenders report different score models on different scales, and choose which loans go into a public pool (CarMax's public CAOT pools are selected from its core portfolio with FICO 650 or above while lower-credit obligors go to CarMax Select, and joint loans average co-borrowers' FICO scores; Exeter's pools since 2025-2 exclude obligors with credit bureau score 640 or above and proprietary score 240 or above, which go to Exeter Select), so the same score is not quite the same borrower from one lender to the next. And the regression discontinuity designs at lenders' own pricing cutoffs returned mostly nulls: half the candidate cutoffs failed a density test, meaning the applicant pool changes at the line, and the survivors were underpowered.

The short version

Issuers with low losses have far fewer accounts that fall behind, in cards and in auto loans. amex's share of balances 30 days late is 0.29 times synchrony's, and its loss rate is 0.29 times. Both stories predict that. They differ on what happens to an account once it is late, and public card filings do not show it, so these filings do not answer the question in the title. In auto loans, where each loan can be followed, the prime lenders' advantage at the same score is mostly fewer loans falling behind, and for some of them a smaller part is in how late loans end.
