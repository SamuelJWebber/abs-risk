Card trusts: charge-off against what the score mix predicts

Built 2026-11-09 from monthly 10-D reports and prospectus composition tables on EDGAR, and CFPB card market report figure data. 6 trusts, 94 calendar months.

Trust pools are selected, seasoned accounts, not the issuer's book. The FICO mix is a snapshot from the latest prospectus and is held fixed between prospectus dates. Charge-off bases differ by trust and are kept as reported. The residual is actual minus what the pool's score mix predicts under a stated loss-by-tier shape; it mixes underwriting, product, customer mix within a score band, pool selection and behaviour, and it cannot say which. Nothing here is a payment-hierarchy estimate.

What the data says

The ends of the ranking hold; the middle does not. synchrony charges off more than its own score mix predicts under every one of the 4 shapes (residual +1.07% to +1.73%); amex charges off least relative to its mix under every shape (-1.39% to +0.40%). 3 of 6 trusts change rank depending on which loss curve is assumed (bofa, chase, comet). So the ordering of the extremes is a finding and the ordering of the middle is not.

The size of the gap is not identified. For 5 of 6 trusts (amex, bofa, chase, citi, comet) the residual changes sign with the assumed loss curve: the same trust looks worse than its mix under the consumer-level shapes and better under the card-level ones. The two families disagree by about five times on how much more a deep subprime account loses than a prime one, and that choice moves every level. Only synchrony (+1.07% to +1.73%) keeps the same sign under all 4, so it is the one trust whose gap does not depend on the assumption.

How much of the spread is score mix depends entirely on the shape. Charge-off across these trusts spans 1.83% to 4.97%, a factor of 2.7. The mix alone predicts a spread of only 1.2 times under acms2018 and 2.4 times under fico_fed2007. On the flatter card-level curves the pools look nearly identical and almost none of the difference is composition; on the steeper consumer curves most of it is. Public data does not settle which curve a card portfolio actually follows.

For scale, below-prime receivables run from 5.4% at amex to 23.9% at synchrony. Every one of these pools is overwhelmingly prime and above, which is what a public card trust is: the securitised, seasoned end of an issuer's book, not the book.

5. Does anyone pay this card first?

A trust with a low loss rate either has accounts that rarely go delinquent, or accounts that go delinquent and do not end in a loss. Those are different claims. The monthly charge-off rate factors exactly, with no modelling, into three shares the trust reports itself:

monthly charge-off  =  entry (30+ share)  x  progression (90+ / 30+)  x  conversion (monthly charge-off / 90+)

If cardholders really did protect one issuer's account, that issuer's conversion would be low: accounts would reach 90 days late and still be rescued. Selection instead shows up in entry. The comparison of conversion is the more trustworthy half, because it conditions on accounts already 90 days late rather than on the pool's score mix.

The advantage is in never getting into trouble, not in surviving it. amex loses 0.37 times what synchrony loses each month. Its 30-plus share is 0.29 times synchrony's, but once an account is 90 days late it converts to a loss at 1.38 times synchrony's rate. Entry accounts for 124% of the gap and conversion for -33%. For amex, bofa, chase the entry share exceeds one, meaning conversion works against them: their deeply delinquent accounts end in loss more readily than the worst trust's do, and the entry advantage has to cover that too. That is the opposite of what protection would look like. If cardholders paid this issuer first, accounts would reach 90 days late and then be rescued, which is a low conversion rate, not a high one. Two other readings survive: the issuer may charge off faster by policy or re-age less, and the pools differ in score mix. The mix objection does not touch conversion, which already conditions on being 90 days late; the policy objection does.

The same ratio through four very different years

If borrowers rank their debts, the ranking should bite hardest when money is tight, so a protected card's 90-day-late accounts should be rescued more often in a squeeze and its conversion ratio should fall. This is the one place the two stories make opposite predictions about a direction, rather than about a level, which is why it is worth looking at even though the periods are crude.

Between pre-covid 2019 and squeeze 2023H2 on, delinquency itself moved by about 1.6 times, so households really were squeezed and relieved across this window. The conversion ratios did not fall for anyone who looks protected. They rose for amex, bofa, chase and fell for 0 trust(s). Prioritisation predicts a particular sign here: a card that borrowers pay first should rescue more of its 90-day-late accounts exactly when money is tight, so its conversion ratio should drop in the squeeze. amex, the trust with the lowest loss rate, moves +0.35 over the window, the wrong way for that story. The trust with the lowest conversion throughout is citi, which is not the lowest-loss trust, so the ordering by 'accounts that survive trouble' does not match the ordering by 'accounts that lose least'. Taken together the cycle evidence does not support borrowers ranking these issuers; it fits fixed differences in who was lent to and in how each issuer charges off.

6. Panel regression

Trust-month regression of the gross charge-off rate on tier shares with month fixed effects, then with trust fixed effects added, Driscoll-Kraay standard errors. Whether either can say anything depends on how the tier shares vary, and they come from prospectus snapshots: one per trust in this build.

month_fe: saturated. 6 distinct tier mixes across 6 trusts for 5 tier coefficients plus an intercept: the fit reproduces each trust's mean exactly, so the coefficients re-express the trust means and are not a mix gradient.

month_and_trust_fe: not identified. no trust's mix changes over the sample (one prospectus snapshot each), so the tier shares are collinear with the trust effects.

So this section has no coefficient to report. A score-mix gradient across card trusts needs more trusts than tiers, or mixes that move within a trust over time; six trusts with one snapshot each give neither. The shape-times-level residual (section 3) and the stage decomposition (section 5) do not depend on it. The fitted numbers are in a2_panel.csv for anyone who wants to see what a saturated fit looks like.

Until 2026-11-09 this section printed both columns as estimates, including trust-effect coefficients in the billions from the unidentified specification.
