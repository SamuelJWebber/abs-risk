Card trusts: charge-off against what the score mix predicts

Data through September 2026. Monthly 10-D reports and prospectus composition tables on EDGAR, and CFPB card market report figure data: 6 trusts, 94 calendar months, December 2018 to September 2026.

Trust pools are selected, seasoned accounts, not the issuer's book. Charge-off bases differ by trust and are kept as reported. The residual is actual minus what the pool's score mix predicts under a stated loss-by-tier shape; it mixes underwriting, product, customer mix within a score band, pool selection and behaviour, and it cannot say which. In principle it says whether a trust loses more than its score mix predicts, by how many points, and since when. Here the number of points depends on which loss curve is assumed (section 4), so the page reports a sign only where every curve agrees, and the years in which it held. It cannot say why. Nothing here is a payment-hierarchy estimate. The score mix is one prospectus snapshot per trust, dated March 2025 to July 2026, applied to every month since December 2018. So 92% of trust-months are predicted from a snapshot dated after them, and when a trust files a new prospectus every earlier month's prediction for it changes. The CFPB industry level runs to 2024, so months in 2025-26 use the 2024 level (22% of trust-months).

What the data says

The ends of the ranking hold; the middle does not. synchrony charges off more than its own score mix predicts under every one of the 4 shapes (residual +1.07% to +1.73%); amex charges off least relative to its mix under every shape (-1.39% to +0.41%). 3 of 6 trusts change rank depending on which loss curve is assumed (bofa, chase, comet). So the ordering of the extremes is a finding and the ordering of the middle is not.

The size of the gap is not identified. For 5 of 6 trusts (amex, bofa, chase, citi, comet) the residual changes sign with the assumed loss curve: the same trust looks worse than its mix under the consumer-level shapes and better under the card-level ones. The two families disagree by about five times on how much more a deep subprime account loses than a prime one, and that choice moves every level. Only synchrony (+1.07% to +1.73%) keeps the same sign under all 4, so it is the one trust whose gap does not depend on the assumption.

How much of the spread is score mix depends entirely on the shape. Charge-off across these trusts spans 1.83% to 4.97%, a factor of 2.7. The mix alone predicts a spread of only 1.2 times under acms2018 and 2.4 times under fico_fed2007. On the flatter card-level curves the pools look nearly identical and almost none of the difference is composition; on the steeper consumer curves most of it is. Public data does not settle which curve a card portfolio actually follows.

For scale, below-prime receivables run from 5.4% at amex to 23.9% at synchrony. Every one of these pools is overwhelmingly prime and above, which is what a public card trust is: the securitised, seasoned end of an issuer's book, not the book.

The ranking changes with the shape in the middle, not at the ends. amex, citi, synchrony keep the same rank under every shape; the others move. So the ordering of the trusts that hold their place is reported as a finding and the ordering of the rest is not.

The straddle rule. Buckets that straddle a tier edge are spread uniformly in score. Putting every such bucket wholly in its lowest tier, or wholly in its highest, bounds any rule that keeps a bucket's balances inside its own edges. Under those bounds the mean residuals move by up to 1.47 percentage points, most under the steep consumer-level shapes, and the rank changes for amex, bofa, chase, citi, comet under at least one shape; synchrony keeps the same rank under every shape and every rule. So the straddle rule is a second assumption, beside the loss curve, that moves the size of a residual; the sign and rank statements above are made under the uniform rule. Every rank is in a1_straddle.csv.

5. Does anyone pay this card first?

A trust with a low loss rate either has accounts that rarely go delinquent, or accounts that go delinquent and do not end in a loss. Those are different claims. The monthly charge-off rate factors exactly, with no modelling, into three shares the trust reports itself:

monthly charge-off  =  entry (30+ share)  x  progression (90+ / 30+)  x  conversion (monthly charge-off / 90+)

If cardholders really did protect one issuer's account, that issuer's conversion would be low: accounts would reach 90 days late and still be rescued. Selection instead shows up in entry. The comparison of conversion is the more trustworthy half, because it conditions on accounts already 90 days late rather than on the pool's score mix.

Each column is a geometric mean over the trust's months, so entry x progression x conversion is co_monthly exactly and the three shares of the gap sum to one. Ratios and shares are against the trust with the highest loss rate.

The advantage is in never getting into trouble, not in surviving it. amex loses 0.37 times what synchrony loses each month. Its 30-plus share is 0.29 times synchrony's, but once an account is 90 days late it converts to a loss at 1.39 times synchrony's rate. Entry accounts for 125% of the gap, progression for +8% and conversion for -33%. Progression barely differs between trusts (0.47 to 0.51 of 30-day-late balances reach 90 days), so the story is in the other two. For amex, bofa, chase the entry share exceeds one, meaning conversion works against them: their deeply delinquent accounts end in loss more readily than the worst trust's do, and the entry advantage has to cover that too. That is the opposite of what protection would look like. If cardholders paid this issuer first, accounts would reach 90 days late and then be rescued, which is a low conversion rate, not a high one. Two other readings survive: the issuer may charge off faster by policy or re-age less, and the pools differ in score mix. The mix objection does not touch conversion, which already conditions on being 90 days late; the policy objection does. The trust whose 90-day-late balances convert least is citi, not the lowest-loss trust, so the ordering by surviving trouble does not match the ordering by losing least.

The direction test: does conversion fall when money is tight?

If borrowers rank their debts, the ranking should bite hardest when money is tight, so a protected card's 90-day-late accounts should be rescued more often then and its conversion ratio should fall. This is the one place the two stories make opposite predictions about a direction, rather than about a level. It needs months that really were tight, so they are picked by the trusts' own delinquency: each trust's 30-plus share over its own average, averaged across trusts.

Split the 94 months by the trusts' own delinquency. In the 47 months when it ran above its median (1.16 times each trust's usual 30-plus share, against 0.84 in the other 47), the conversion ratio against synchrony fell for amex (-0.10), rose for citi (+0.09), and moved by less than 0.05 for bofa, chase and comet. For amex, the lowest-loss trust, it went from 1.44 in loose months to 1.34 in tight ones: the direction prioritisation predicts, while staying above synchrony's 1.00. Of the named periods, the step from normalising 2022-23H1 to squeeze 2023H2 on raised every trust's 30-plus share (by 16% to 56%). Across it the conversion ratio fell for bofa (-0.12) and comet (-0.06), rose for none, and was flat for amex, chase and citi. The first and last named periods are not a tightening: every trust's 30-plus share is lower in squeeze 2023H2 on than in pre-covid 2019 (0.74 to 0.98 times). Until November 2026 this page compared exactly those two and read the result as a test of prioritisation; it was not one. So the direction test is mixed. The moves either way are small beside the level gap above, and conversion divides a flow by a stock that builds ahead of the charge-offs, which lowers it whenever delinquency is rising, whoever is paid first. The level comparison carries the argument; this test neither confirms nor overturns it.

6. Panel regression

Trust-month regression of the gross charge-off rate on tier shares with month fixed effects, then with trust fixed effects added, Driscoll-Kraay standard errors. Whether either can say anything depends on how the tier shares vary, and they come from prospectus snapshots: one per trust in this build.

month_fe: saturated. 6 distinct tier mixes across 6 trusts for 5 tier coefficients plus an intercept: the fit reproduces each trust's mean exactly, so the coefficients re-express the trust means and are not a mix gradient.

month_and_trust_fe: not identified. no trust's mix changes over the sample (one prospectus snapshot each), so the tier shares are collinear with the trust effects.

So this section has no coefficient to report. A score-mix gradient across card trusts needs more trusts than tiers, or mixes that move within a trust over time; six trusts with one snapshot each give neither. The shape-times-level residual (section 3) and the stage decomposition (section 5) do not depend on it. The fitted numbers are in a2_panel.csv for anyone who wants to see what a saturated fit looks like.

Until 2026-11-09 this section printed both columns as estimates, including trust-effect coefficients in the billions from the unidentified specification.
