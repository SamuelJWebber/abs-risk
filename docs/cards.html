Card trusts: charge-off against what the score mix predicts

Data through October 2026. Monthly 10-D reports and prospectus composition tables on EDGAR, and CFPB card market report figure data: 6 trusts, 95 calendar months, December 2018 to October 2026.

Trust pools are selected, seasoned accounts, not the issuer's book. Every trust is compared on its net charge-off rate, losses after recoveries, because that is the only rate all six print: Citi's monthly report gives no gross rate. Until November 2026 this page labelled Citi's net rate gross and set it against the other five trusts' gross rates. Annualisation bases differ by trust and are kept as reported. The residual is actual minus what the pool's score mix predicts under a stated loss-by-tier shape; it mixes underwriting, product, customer mix within a score band, pool selection and behaviour, and it cannot say which. In principle it says whether a trust loses more than its score mix predicts, by how many points, and since when. Here the number of points depends on which loss curve is assumed (section 4), so the page reports a sign only where every curve agrees, and the years in which it held. It cannot say why. Nothing here is a payment-hierarchy estimate. The score mix is one prospectus snapshot per trust, dated March 2025 to August 2026, applied to every month since December 2018. So 92% of trust-months are predicted from a snapshot dated after them, and when a trust files a new prospectus every earlier month's prediction for it changes. The CFPB industry level runs to 2024, so months in 2025-26 use the 2024 level (23% of trust-months).

What the data says

The ends of the ranking hold under most assumptions, not under all of them. Across the 12 combinations of loss curve and straddle rule (section 4), synchrony has the largest residual in 10 and amex the smallest in 10; 6 of 6 trusts change rank somewhere among them. synchrony loses more than its own score mix predicts in all 12: its residual is +0.01% to +1.48% after recoveries, so it is positive before them too. So the two ends are close to a finding, not quite one, and the ordering between them is not a finding at all. One more assumption sits under that sign, and the curves do not cover it: synchrony's pool is set against the general-purpose industry level like the other five, though about a quarter of its receivables are private label cards, which charge off at roughly twice the general-purpose rate industry-wide. A review on 2026-11-09 found the sign survives at that blend and reverses if the whole pool is treated as private label.

The size of the gap is not identified. Measured like for like against the CFPB's gross industry rate, for 5 of 6 trusts (amex, bofa, chase, citi, comet) the residual changes sign with the assumed loss curve: the same trust looks worse than its mix under the consumer-level shapes and better under the card-level ones. The two families disagree by about five times on how much more a deep subprime account loses than a prime one, and that choice moves every level. Only synchrony (+1.07% to +1.74%) keeps the same sign under all 4, so it is the one trust whose sign does not depend on the loss curve.

How much of the spread is score mix depends entirely on the shape. Charge-off across these trusts spans 1.20% to 4.01%, a factor of 3.3. The mix alone predicts a spread of only 1.2 times under acms2018 and 2.4 times under fico_fed2007. On the flatter card-level curves the pools look nearly identical and almost none of the difference is composition; on the steeper consumer curves most of it is. Public data does not settle which curve a card portfolio actually follows.

For scale, below-prime receivables run from 5.4% at amex to 23.7% at synchrony. Every one of these pools is overwhelmingly prime and above, which is what a public card trust is: the securitised, seasoned end of an issuer's book, not the book.

The ranking changes with the shape. Under the uniform straddle rule, chase, synchrony keep the same rank under every shape and the others move. The next paragraph widens the rule, and the headline counts both.

The straddle rule. Buckets that straddle a tier edge are spread uniformly in score. Putting every such bucket wholly in its lowest tier, or wholly in its highest, bounds any rule that keeps a bucket's balances inside its own edges. Under those bounds the mean residuals move by up to 1.48 percentage points, most under the steep consumer-level shapes, and the rank changes for amex, bofa, chase, citi, comet, synchrony under at least one shape; no trust keeps one rank under every shape and every rule. So the straddle rule is a second assumption, beside the loss curve, that moves the size of a residual; the sign and rank statements above are made under the uniform rule. Every rank is in a1_straddle.csv.

5. Does anyone pay this card first?

A trust with a low loss rate either has accounts that rarely go delinquent, or accounts that go delinquent and do not end in a loss. Those are different claims. The monthly charge-off rate factors exactly, with no modelling, into three shares the trust reports itself:

monthly charge-off  =  entry (30+ share)  x  progression (90+ / 30+)  x  conversion (monthly net charge-off / 90+)

The split is exact arithmetic. What it can carry is narrower than this page used to claim: entry shows how much of a trust's advantage is fewer late balances, and conversion does not show what becomes of an account once it is late, for the reason given under the table.

Each column is a geometric mean over the trust's months, so entry x progression x conversion is co_monthly exactly and the three shares of the gap sum to one. Ratios and shares are against the trust with the highest loss rate.

The low-loss trusts have far fewer late balances. What happens to an account once it is late, these filings cannot say. amex loses 0.29 times what synchrony loses each month, and its 30-plus share is 0.29 times synchrony's. Entry accounts for 101% of the gap, progression for +6% and conversion for -7%. Progression barely differs between trusts (0.47 to 0.51 of 30-day-late balances are 90 days late). Fewer late balances is what selection predicts. It is also what a card that people pay first would show, because paying it first keeps it from falling behind. So the entry gap is large and real, and it does not separate the two stories.

Conversion is not the fate of late accounts. It divides every charge-off in the month by the balances then 90 days late, and a charge-off need not have been among them. The prospectuses state the policy: an account is written off at about 180 days late, or sooner when the cardholder is bankrupt or has died (American Express, Citi, Chase and Synchrony each say so in the prospectuses used here). The monthly reports show how often the sooner route is taken. An account can reach the 180-day write-off only from the last delinquency bucket of the month before, yet chase wrote off 1.21 times the dollars that were in that bucket a month earlier, over 94 months, and more than the bucket held in 99% of them. At least 17% of its charge-offs therefore never aged through late delinquency. The same lower bound elsewhere: synchrony 5%, bofa 3%. amex prints its last 2 monthly cohorts as one bucket (120+ days): its charge-offs were 0.69 of the whole bucket, which is 1.37 times one cohort if the cohorts are equal, a lower bound of 27%. The bucket counts finance charges and fees and the charge-off is principal only, so each ratio understates. citi is on its net rate, which understates further.

Until November 2026 this page read a conversion ratio above one as the opposite of protection. That reading is withdrawn.

The direction test: does conversion fall when money is tight?

If borrowers rank their debts, the ranking should bite hardest when money is tight, so a protected card's late accounts should be rescued more often then and its conversion ratio should fall. It needs months that really were tight, so they are picked by the trusts' own delinquency: each trust's 30-plus share over its own average, averaged across trusts. Conversion is a blunt instrument for this, as the paragraph above says, so read the result as a description.

Split the 95 months by the trusts' own delinquency. In the 47 months when it ran above its median (1.16 times each trust's usual 30-plus share, against 0.85 in the other 48), the conversion ratio against synchrony fell for no trust, rose for amex (+0.08) and comet (+0.09), and moved by less than 0.05 for bofa, chase and citi. For amex, the lowest-loss trust, it went from 1.05 in loose months to 1.13 in tight ones: the wrong way for prioritisation, while staying above synchrony's 1.00. Of the named periods, the step from normalising 2022-23H1 to squeeze 2023H2 on raised every trust's 30-plus share (by 16% to 56%). Across it the conversion ratio fell for citi (-0.06), rose for amex (+0.08) and comet (+0.09), and was flat for bofa and chase. The first and last named periods are not a tightening: every trust's 30-plus share is lower in squeeze 2023H2 on than in pre-covid 2019 (0.74 to 0.98 times). Until November 2026 this page compared exactly those two and read the result as a test of prioritisation; it was not one. No trust's conversion ratio fell in the tight months, which is not the sign prioritisation predicts. Conversion divides a flow by a stock that builds ahead of the charge-offs, which lowers it whenever delinquency is rising, whoever is paid first, and it moves with the share of charge-offs that never aged through late delinquency. So this test is weak evidence in either direction and settles nothing on its own.

6. Panel regression

Trust-month regression of the net charge-off rate on tier shares with month fixed effects, then with trust fixed effects added, Driscoll-Kraay standard errors. Whether either can say anything depends on how the tier shares vary, and they come from prospectus snapshots: one per trust in this build.

month_fe: saturated. 6 distinct tier mixes across 6 trusts for 5 tier coefficients plus an intercept: the fit reproduces each trust's mean exactly, so the coefficients re-express the trust means and are not a mix gradient.

month_and_trust_fe: not identified. no trust's mix changes over the sample (one prospectus snapshot each), so the tier shares are collinear with the trust effects.

So this section has no coefficient to report. A score-mix gradient across card trusts needs more trusts than tiers, or mixes that move within a trust over time; six trusts with one snapshot each give neither. The shape-times-level residual (section 3) and the stage decomposition (section 5) do not depend on it. The fitted numbers are in a2_panel.csv for anyone who wants to see what a saturated fit looks like.

Until 2026-11-09 this section printed both columns as estimates, including trust-effect coefficients in the billions from the unidentified specification.
